# 1. The Game and the Dice

Dawn Wars is a tabletop battle game for two players. Each of you commands an army of model
soldiers, monsters and war machines, formed into units. You take turns to move your units,
shoot, cast spells and fight, using the rules in this book and a handful of dice. The battle
lasts a set number of rounds, and then you work out who has won.

This chapter covers the basics that every other chapter relies on: measuring, dice, templates,
the order of a game, which way models face and what they can see, and how models are formed
into units.

## Measuring

- All distances are in inches ("). Use a tape measure or ruler.
- You may measure any distance at any time.
- Measure between the nearest edges of two bases: from the closest point of one model's base
  to the closest point of the other thing (a model, a terrain feature, and so on).
- Two models are **within** a distance of each other if any part of one base is that close to
  the other base. If any part of your model's base is 6" or less from an enemy model's base,
  the two are within 6" of each other.
- If your models stand on a movement tray, ignore the tray. Always measure from the bases of
  the models themselves.

## Dice

### The D6

Almost every roll in the game uses an ordinary six-sided die, numbered 1 to 6, called a
**D6**. Many dice show a symbol in place of the 6; it still counts as a 6.

- **Roll a D6:** roll one die.
- **Target number rolls:** a number followed by a plus sign is the score you need. A roll of
  **4+** succeeds on a 4, 5 or 6, and fails on a 1, 2 or 3.
- **2D6, 3D6 and so on:** roll that many D6 and add them up.
- **D3:** you don't need a three-sided die. Roll a D6, halve the score and round up. So 1-2
  gives 1, 3-4 gives 2 and 5-6 gives 3.

### Artillery dice and the Scatter dice

Two special dice are used, most often together for war machines:

| Die | Faces |
|---|---|
| **Artillery dice** | 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and Misfire |
| **Scatter dice** | arrows, and Hit! symbols |

The Scatter dice may also be rolled with one or more D6, to give a random direction and a
random distance.

### Modifiers

A rule may tell you to add to or take away from a roll. Roll first, then apply the modifier to
change the score.

- If a rule says to divide a roll, round any fraction up unless that rule says otherwise.
- Halve or multiply first. Add and subtract modifiers after that.

### Natural rolls

A **natural** roll is the number the die actually shows, before any modifier is applied.

### Re-rolls

Some rules let you **re-roll** a die: pick it up and throw it again.

- The second result stands, even if it is worse.
- A die can never be re-rolled more than once, however many rules would allow it.
- When you re-roll a roll made of several dice (such as 2D6), you re-roll all of them, unless
  the rule giving the re-roll says otherwise.

### Roll-offs

When a rule calls for a **roll-off**, each player rolls one die (normally a D6). The higher
score wins. On a tie, both roll again, unless a rule tells you otherwise.

## Templates

Some weapons and spells hit an area. They use a template:

| Template | Shape |
|---|---|
| **Blast template** | a circle, 3" or 5" across |
| **Flame template** | a teardrop, about 8" long |

The rule for the attack tells you where to put the template and whether it can scatter.

### Who is hit

Once the template is in its final place, look at which bases lie beneath it:

- A model is **hit automatically** if its base is entirely under the template, or if any part
  of its base is under the hole in the middle of a blast template.
- A model whose base is only partly covered is hit if you roll **4+** on a D6.

### Scatter

When a rule tells you to place something and **scatter** it:

1. Put the object where the rule says. Roll the Scatter dice for direction, along with any
   other dice the rule gives for distance in inches.
2. **Hit!** The object stays where it is. Carry on with the rule.
3. **An arrow.** Move the object in the direction of the arrow, as many inches as the other
   dice show. It passes over terrain, units and anything else in the way, unless the rule says
   otherwise. Then carry on with the rule from its new position.

## Moving directly towards or away

Some rules make a unit head **directly towards** a unit or object, or **directly away** from
it. Picture a straight line joining the two centres: the middle of your unit and the middle of
the thing it moves in relation to. Your unit's middle point travels along that line.

## Moving through units

Normally a unit may not move through another unit. A few rules are exceptions. A fleeing unit,
for example, may be forced to pass through another unit, and when a unit manoeuvres, one of
its corners may have no choice but to clip a unit nearby. Where the rules allow it, that is
fine.

## Committing to a choice

You may change your mind about what you are about to do, and should allow your opponent the
same. But once dice have been rolled, or a move has been made, that choice is fixed. You
cannot go back and change anything that happened before the roll or the move.

## Unusual situations

Units in tight formations, pressed up against each other, will now and then produce a
position the rules don't cover cleanly. Settle it in whatever way keeps the game moving, or
ask someone neutral to decide. At an organised event, the event's judge has the final word.

If you still can't agree, the unit simply can't do what was being attempted: it doesn't make
that move, doesn't take that shot, and so on.

## The most important rule

If you and your opponent read a rule differently, look it up together, agree, and play on. If
you can't agree, roll off. The winner's reading applies for the rest of the game. Above every
rule, the point is that both of you have a good game and part as friends.

## The order of a game

A game runs in these stages:

1. **Muster your forces.** Each player needs an army. You can field every model you own, but
   usually players agree a points total and choose their armies to it (chapter 12).
2. **Set up the battlefield.** Any flat surface will do. Place terrain for the armies to fight
   over (chapter 11).
3. **Deploy.** The armies are set up facing each other. The battle you are playing says where
   each player may place their models, and who takes the first turn.
4. **Play.** You take turns. In each turn your army moves, shoots, casts spells and fights
   (chapters 3 to 9). The battle you are playing says how many rounds the game lasts.
5. **Aftermath.** Work out who has won, as the battle you are playing describes. Usually the
   winner is whoever wiped out more of the opposing army; some battles are won by taking
   objectives instead.

## Facing, arcs and line of sight

### Arcs

Most models have a front, two flanks and a rear. Draw lines out from each corner of the base
at 45°. These lines cut the space around the model into four 90° **arcs**: front, left
flank, right flank and rear. A unit's arcs come from the facing of the models in it.

Arcs decide two things: what a model can see, and which side of a unit an enemy charge hits.

### Vision arc

A model can only see things inside its **vision arc**. Unless a rule says otherwise, the
vision arc is 90° wide and is the same as the model's front arc.

### Line of sight

To check whether one model has **line of sight** to another, get down to the first model's
level and look from where it stands.

- If a straight, unbroken line runs from somewhere in its vision arc and touches the
  other model anywhere, it has line of sight.
- If terrain blocks every such line, or every such line passes over or through another model,
  it has no line of sight.
- A model always counts as inside its own vision arc, and can always see itself and its own
  unit.
- A unit can **see** another unit if any one of its models has line of sight to any one model
  of the other unit.

Every model and unit blocks the view. So, as a rule, only the front rank of a unit can
see anything.

### Cover

If terrain or other models block part of the view, the target is **in cover**. Check from the
looking model's position:

| How much of the target is hidden | The target is |
|---|---|
| none of it | in the open |
| half of it or less | in **partial cover** |
| more than half of it | in **full cover** |

When the target is a unit, count how many of its models are hidden; when it is a lone model,
judge how much of that model is hidden. Shooting at a target in partial cover is at -1 to hit,
and in full cover at -2 to hit (chapter 5).

## Forming units

Before the battle, every model in your army is placed in a **unit**, normally when you write
your army list. A unit is usually a group of models of the same kind, set up in one formation.
A single model fighting alone (a character, a chariot, a large monster, a war machine with its
crew) is a unit too. Whenever the rules say "unit", that includes a unit of one model.

### Formations

Every unit has a formation. The formation shapes how the unit moves and fights, and each has
its own rules.

- A unit may take a formation only if it has the special rule of that name (**Close Order**,
  **Open Order**, **Skirmishers**, and so on).
- A unit with more than one of these special rules chooses its formation when it deploys. It
  can switch to another one during the game by reforming (chapter 4).
- Most units fight in Close Order, so the core rules are written around it. Some army lists
  have formations of their own.

### Close Order

A unit in **Close Order** stands in tight ranks:

- It has at least two models, set base to base, edges touching and front corners lined up.
- Every model faces the same way.
- The models are lined up in **ranks** (rows across) and **files** (rows front to back).
- Each rank has the same number of models wherever possible. If not, the short rank is the
  rear one.

From then on the unit moves and fights as one body for the whole battle.

### Open Order

A unit in **Open Order** is laid out exactly as a Close Order unit: two or more models base to
base, edges touching and front corners lined up, all facing one way, in ranks and files of
equal size with any short rank at the back. What sets it apart is how it moves and deals with
terrain (chapter 13).

### Skirmish

A unit in **Skirmish** formation (**Skirmishers**) never forms ranks and files. It moves as a
loose crowd or a ragged line, which lets it travel fast and make use of terrain for
shelter (chapter 13).

### Unit shape

A Close Order unit's shape matters, because it earns bonuses. By its shape the unit is either
in **Combat Order** or in **Marching Column**.

| Shape | Formation |
|---|---|
| more models per rank than per file (wide) | Combat Order |
| as many models per rank as per file (square) | Combat Order |
| more models per file than per rank (deep) | Marching Column |

### Combat Order

A Close Order unit in Combat Order gains two things:

- **Rank Bonus.** If its ranks are wide enough, the unit adds +1 for every rank behind the
  front one, up to a maximum. Both the number of models a rank needs and the maximum bonus
  depend on the unit's troop type (chapter 2). Some special rules raise that maximum. A rear
  rank that isn't full still counts, as long as it has enough models in it.
- **Melee Outcome bonus.** If its Unit Mass is 10 or more, the unit may add +1 to its
  Melee Outcome (chapter 6).

### Marching Column

A Close Order unit in Marching Column:

- can't claim a Rank Bonus;
- can't charge;
- may move three times its Movement when it marches, instead of twice (chapter 4).

### Disrupted units

A **Disrupted** unit has lost its cohesion and can't claim a Rank Bonus. A unit becomes
Disrupted when:

- an enemy unit of Unit Mass 5 or more is engaged with it in its flank or rear; or
- it ends its move with 25% (a quarter) or more of its models in difficult terrain, or
  with the unit across a low linear obstacle (chapter 11).

Enemy Skirmishers never cause Disruption.
